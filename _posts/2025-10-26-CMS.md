As I'm reviewing old class notes and projects such as this one [CMS LOS Prediction](https://data.cms.gov/collection/synthetic-medicare-enrollment-fee-for-service-claims-and-prescription-drug-event), I'm realizing that I always forget exactly what I did, and how much time it would save me to document more as I'm working. I also realized that I didn't actually have justifications for some of the decisions I made regarding why I did what I did to handle my ZID (zero inflated data)  problem. To that end, I did some surface level research on how ZID can be handled, and in doing so I believe that my methods were in line with [what I found](https://www.numberanalytics.com/blog/practical-guide-zero-inflated-models).

Below are general notes on what I actually did, and my reasoning behind the decisions I made.

#### Data Processing
    - merged enrollment, inpatient, and drug encounter datasets into a master set
        - had to identify continuously enrolled beneficiaries
        - check for duplicated entries
        - ensure that every entry corresponded to a single beneficiary + encounter
    - PCA
        - found the 3 principal components explained a majority of the variance in the data
    - Based on my exploratory data analysis I believed that there were structurally different underlying processes that generated the lengths of stays
        - For example, a broken arm is a very different “underlying structural process” than being hit by a car and needing life-saving surgery. Because of this, the probability distributions of the lengths of stay for people with less serious injuries are very different than those for people with more severe injuries.
        - My initial thought was that there would be some kind of way to “rank” these different disease severities however I couldn’t find any pre-existing or official rankings online. In order to deal with this, I decided that instead of creating simple rankings, I would group the data into separate clusters, and then create an individual model for each of these groups. This would allow each model to incorporate the different levels of variability between each of the groups, and better predict the eventual length of stay. It also allowed the groupings to be based off of all the data, and not just disease severity. This is beneficial because it accounts for the fact that there are more variables that determine length of stay, than only what medical problem you have.
        - To create these groups, I wanted to let the data “speak for itself” and assume nothing about the characteristics of the data, so I PCA to ?consolidate? the variability, and then used kmeans clustering (unsupervised method) to create the groups of beneficiaries. Then I split the original dataset based on these group assignments and created a separate random forest regression model for each of the groups.
        - A type of zero inflated model that does not assume the existence of structural zero’s, but allows for their existence through the grouping.
            - If the structural zeros do exist, then it is assumed that one of the clustered groups would capture those zeros, and if they don’t exist, then the groups would also reflect that.
            - This model also makes no assumptions about the probability distribution of the occurrence of both structural and sampling zeros
