One of the projects I'm working on right now is an analysis of the synthetic datasets that are released by the Center for Medicare and Medicaid services [linked here](https://data.cms.gov/collection/synthetic-medicare-enrollment-fee-for-service-claims-and-prescription-drug-event). My main goal is to try to predict the length of stay based on demographic features (age, race, sex, state), total prescription drug payments, number of diagnoses within a given year, presence of end stage renal disease, admission facility type (ER, inpatient, ... etc.) and the most interesting in my opinion, the principal diagnosis code. ## Training Data Choices

This is really what I wanted to write about, to try and clarify the ideas I have about this. 

As far as I see it, there are at least a couple of differen ways of dividing the data into training and test sets. 
1. Ignore the patient-specific effects, and use the most recent year as the test-set, and all other data as the training set. The problem or question I have with this method, is that it treats each observation (which is defined by a beneficiary ID, specific claim ID, and year) as though they are entirely independent from all other observations in the data set which is a big (and incorrect) assumption to make. I also am concerned about how using patient specific metrics, i.e. average claims per year, or diagnoses in a specific year, would affect the conclusions that I could derive using this method of data seperation. This method feels as though I'm missing out on some kind of information, or as though I'm making an assumption I can't but I'm not sure. 

2. Just use the previous year to predict the following year, i.e. use only data from 2016 to predict 2017. This method obviously leaves out so much data however, that I don't think it's worth it.

I think the first method is the most viable of the two, and I think the main assumption I'd be making with this method is that that the observations are independent in regards to patient ID and year.
